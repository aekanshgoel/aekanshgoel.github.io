You know that moment when a meeting ends, your calendar says it is time to get back to work… but your mind is not there yet?

You open the document you were supposed to finish.

You read the same line twice.

You check Slack. Get water. Answer one email. Think about something someone said in the meeting.

Then, a few minutes later, you finally feel like yourself again.

If that happens to you, you are not lazy. And you are not necessarily bad at focusing.

You may simply be experiencing the transition cost of a meeting.

Some people call this “Meeting Recovery Syndrome.” It is a catchy name, but it is not an official diagnosis or a formal scientific term.

Researchers use more careful language, such as meeting recovery, meeting fatigue, or meeting-to-work transition time.

The idea is simple:

A meeting can end on your calendar before it has fully ended in your mind.

## Meetings take more than time

We often think of meetings as taking exactly as long as the invitation says.

Thirty minutes. One hour. Done.

But some meetings take more than their scheduled time because they leave something behind.

Maybe you are holding a new decision in your head.

Maybe you are trying to remember your action item.

Maybe the conversation was unclear, tense, or frustrating.

Maybe you were expected to speak, persuade, present, manage conflict, or keep your camera on while trying to look engaged.

Maybe the meeting was productive—but now you need a moment to figure out what it means for your actual work.

That mental “after-effect” is what people often feel as post-meeting fog.

## Switching gears is real work

One reason meetings can feel draining is that they require us to switch contexts.

During a meeting, your attention may be on other people, shared decisions, questions, politics, updates, or problems.

Then the meeting ends, and suddenly you are expected to move straight into a detailed spreadsheet, a report, a design task, or deep problem-solving.

That is a big shift.

Research on task switching and interruptions suggests that returning to a task after a disruption can come with a cost. It may take extra effort to remember where you left off, rebuild your train of thought, and fully settle back into the work.

This does not mean every meeting is harmful.

It means your mind may need a moment to change gears.

Think of it like driving. You can turn quickly, but a sharp turn still requires attention.

## Not every meeting feels the same

We have all had meetings that gave us energy.

A good conversation can create clarity. It can help a team solve a problem. It can make you feel heard, connected, and ready to move.

And we have all had the other kind.

The meeting that starts late.

The one where nobody knows why they are there.

The one with 12 people, three side conversations, no decision, and a promise to “circle back.”

Those meetings can feel exhausting not simply because they take time, but because they create confusion, frustration, or more work without giving enough clarity in return.

Research on workplace meetings suggests that quality matters. Meetings that feel relevant, well managed, and useful tend to have better outcomes. Counterproductive meeting behavior—such as poor structure, distractions, unclear goals, or disrespectful interaction—has been linked with lower engagement and more emotional exhaustion.

So the real question is not always, “How many meetings do we have?”

Sometimes it is, “What kind of meetings are we having?”

But the post-meeting fog often gets worse when there is no space between one meeting and the next. That is where back-to-back meetings become more than a calendar problem.
