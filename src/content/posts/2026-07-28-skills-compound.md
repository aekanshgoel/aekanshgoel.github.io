I have watched talented people plateau for years, and it is rarely for lack
of ability. The learning curve for any real skill — an engineering discipline,
a programming language, a way of thinking about problems — is flat for a long
stretch, then curves away. Almost everyone quits in the flat stretch, because
the flat stretch feels like evidence of failure.

It is not. It is the price of admission.

## Practice with a target

Random repetition does not compound; targeted practice does. The difference
is small to describe and hard to keep up:

- **Pick one problem to solve**, not one topic to “study.” When I was
  learning data analysis, I did not take courses. I picked one messy machine
  dataset and refused to stop until I could explain every anomaly in it.
- **Get feedback fast.** A mistake you find yourself is a lesson. A mistake
  you find in six months is a habit. Review your own work the day after, ask
  someone who knows, or run the numbers again and see if you get the same
  answer.
- **Use it twice.** If you solve a problem a second time, write down the
  method in five lines. The second time is where understanding settles, and
  the note is what makes it transferable.

## The note is the asset

> You are not building a body of knowledge. You are building a set of
> reflexes you can reach for under pressure — and a paper trail that proves
> you can.

Nobody reads your notes. That is the point. They are for the future you who
meets the problem at 6 a.m. in a shift meeting and needs the method, not the
lesson. The reflex beats the memory every time, especially on the days that
matter.

## A career is a portfolio of small bets

No one plans a career; they accumulate them. Each project is a small bet that
pays out in options — a skill, a relationship, a reputation for finishing
things. The compounding is quiet and unglamorous, which is why it is durable.

So the plan I actually run is embarrassingly simple: one target at a time,
feedback before pride, notes written down, and no more than one abandoned
thing at a stretch. Boring, survivable, and it has a way of looking
impressive from five years out.
